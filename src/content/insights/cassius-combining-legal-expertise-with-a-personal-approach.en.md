Legal expertise is essential, but so is understanding the people and businesses behind each case. Through Cassius, Stéphanie Cassimon combines legal knowledge with a pragmatic, client-focused approach, helping entrepreneurs, businesses and individuals navigate complex legal matters while keeping their wider interests in mind.

With experience gained in New York and Belgium, an international professional network and a particular expertise in equine law, Stéphanie believes that good legal advice goes beyond resolving immediate issues. It is about building trust, anticipating challenges and finding solutions that work in practice.

As part of the MCSC Group network, Cassius brings valuable legal expertise to a wider international advisory environment. We spoke with Stéphanie about her professional journey, her approach to legal practice, the specific challenges of the equestrian world and the importance of thinking ahead.

##### — Could you tell us about your professional journey and what led you to establish Cassius? What vision did you want to bring to life from the outset?

After completing my studies in New York, I worked at two law firms in Antwerp, where I learned a great deal. I had never planned to establish my own firm. Cassius came about quite organically in the summer of 2024, when I met the right clients at the right time.

Shortly afterwards, I attended my first AIJA (International Association of Young Lawyers) congress in Madrid, which brought together around 700 lawyers from all over the world. It was a real turning point for me. I realised how much I wanted to collaborate with fellow lawyers from different countries. This international outlook therefore became an important part of my vision for Cassius very quickly.

There is also an element of luck involved: being entrusted with cases that have an international dimension and allow these collaborations to become a reality. You can nurture an international outlook and build professional relationships, but opportunities to put them into practice also emerge through the cases you handle and the people you meet.

These encounters and opportunities have helped shape Cassius. Beyond that, however, the central element of my work remains the relationship with the people who entrust me with their cases. I want every client to feel that they, and their matter, are at the centre of my attention.

For me, legal support is built on trust and adapted to the way each client wishes to be involved. Some prefer to follow their case closely and work with me at every stage. Others would rather entrust me with its full management. Both approaches work perfectly well for me. What matters is understanding their perspective, expectations and priorities from the outset. This allows me to direct my work towards their objectives, as far as the legal possibilities and the realities of the case allow.

My role is to explain the available options, the implications and the risks clearly, so that clients can make informed decisions without feeling alone when faced with legal complexity. Beyond defending their interests, I want to provide them with as much peace of mind as possible, particularly when a dispute disrupts their everyday lives.

I want this personal, practical relationship built on trust to become the hallmark of Cassius.

##### — You combine legal expertise with a pragmatic approach focused on business needs. What does this mean in practice, and how does it shape the way you work with clients?

For me, a pragmatic approach means looking beyond the legal aspects of a question and understanding what it actually means for the client's business. A solution may be legally sound without necessarily being the best fit for their priorities, resources or the way their company operates.

In practice, this means considering the different options in context: what outcome are we trying to achieve, within what timeframe, at what cost, and with what potential consequences for the future of the business? My role is to make these choices understandable and recommend a course of action that is not only legally sound but also workable in the client's actual circumstances.

When a difficulty arises, I therefore want to help clients regain the time and peace of mind they need to focus on what matters to them: their business or, in the equestrian sector, their horses.

It is this combination of legal rigour and an understanding of real-world circumstances that guides the way I work.

##### — Cassius advises entrepreneurs and businesses on a range of legal matters. What are the most common challenges your clients face, and when should they seek legal advice before a situation develops into a dispute?

One issue I encounter quite frequently, particularly in family businesses and the equestrian world, is the absence of a written contract or the existence of very basic agreements whose terms are not sufficiently clear.

Trust and personal relationships can create the impression that there is no need to formalise arrangements. As long as everything is going well, the parties do not necessarily see a reason to do so.

However, when a relationship deteriorates, or when the person with whom the original relationship of trust was built is no longer involved and someone else takes over — for example, the next generation — these ambiguities can lead to all kinds of disagreements about what was actually agreed.

In a family business, professional and emotional considerations often overlap. Clarifying roles, expectations and commitments can therefore also help preserve personal relationships. Putting things in writing is not a sign of mistrust; it gives the relationship a clear framework.

That is why I recommend consulting a lawyer before a problem arises, precisely to help prevent it. Legal support is not limited to resolving disputes; its preventive role is equally important.

In practical terms, this may involve establishing a sound contractual framework for a client's recurring transactions or business relationships, and then adapting it to each specific situation, preferably in consultation with their lawyer.

The idea is not to reuse a template automatically without considering the circumstances, but to have a clear document suited to the business, which can be adjusted to reflect the particularities of each relationship. Clarifying expectations and commitments from the outset helps reduce the risk of disagreements later on.

##### — Commercial disputes can be costly, not only financially but also in terms of time and professional relationships. How do you approach conflict resolution, and when do you favour negotiation or an amicable settlement?

When a conflict arises, I look at what the client wants to achieve and the different ways of getting there. I attach considerable importance to finding an amicable solution whenever it serves the client's interests.

A settlement can save legal costs, but also a great deal of time and energy. In some cases, it can help preserve a professional relationship or protect a client's reputation.

This requires an honest conversation: what is the client prepared to accept in order to regain peace of mind and avoid years of legal proceedings, with all the uncertainty that comes with them? Their peace of mind has value too.

My role is to help them weigh the concessions they might make against what legal proceedings could realistically achieve, so that they can make an informed decision.

However, I do not seek compromise at any cost. When legal proceedings are necessary — for example, in a case involving serious indications of fraud by the opposing party — I commit fully to the matter and use all appropriate legal means to defend my client's interests.

For some clients, the issue goes beyond financial considerations. They want what they have experienced to be recognised and they want justice. There may also be a broader, more idealistic dimension: the desire to prevent others from being subjected to the same conduct.

Taking a firm position can also be part of a pragmatic approach, provided the client understands the prospects, costs and risks involved.

It is therefore neither about negotiating in every situation nor about taking legal action as a matter of principle. It is about choosing the approach that best fits the case and what the client genuinely wants to defend.

##### — Contracts are often treated as formal documents rather than strategic tools. What should entrepreneurs pay particular attention to when negotiating or signing a contract?

When I review a contract, the first clauses I look at are those identifying the competent courts and the applicable law.

These are sometimes provisions that receive little attention at the time of signing, even though they can have a significant impact on how a potential dispute will be handled.

I advise my clients to stand firm on these two points and, wherever possible, insist on their own courts and their own legal system being chosen. They should not regard these provisions as minor concessions to be made simply to reach an agreement.

##### — You have developed particular expertise in equine law. What attracted you to this field, and what makes legal matters in the equestrian world distinctive?

I was able to develop this area of practice thanks to the trust of my first clients, who were already well established in the equestrian world, and the other people who opened doors for me in this sector. I am deeply grateful to them.

Beyond the legal expertise itself, this journey also has a very personal dimension. It allows me to bring together my profession and my love of horses, while maintaining a connection with my father, who passed away when I was four, and with the passion that meant so much to him.

I had never imagined that my career would take this direction, but seeing my personal story and professional life come together in this way means a great deal to me.

As for the particularities of the equestrian world, as I mentioned earlier, trust and a person's word carry considerable weight. A handshake can mean a great deal. This is one of the beautiful aspects of these relationships, but unfortunately, trust is not always justified.

And even when it is, it does not necessarily prevent misunderstandings when difficulties arise or when the original people involved are no longer there and others take over.

The challenge is therefore to preserve that trust while ensuring that commitments are clearly defined enough for the relationship to withstand such changes.

##### — From horse sales and breeding agreements to disputes involving riders, owners and stables, what are the most common legal risks in this sector, and which ones are often underestimated?

A horse is a living being whose health and performance can change over time.

When a horse is sold, it is important to distinguish between the qualities expected by the buyer, the information communicated to them and what has actually been agreed regarding the horse's intended use — including whether it may be used for breeding after its sporting career.

Agreements on this subject also deserve to be clearly defined: who will have the right to make decisions, what rights each party will retain, and how the costs and any potential income from breeding activities will be shared.

Finally, I believe that the importance of keeping a record of information and decisions throughout the relationship is sometimes underestimated. This may include discussions about the horse's condition, veterinary records, approval of certain expenses or the involvement of an intermediary.

When several people are involved in the care and management of the same horse, being able to establish exactly what happened can be just as important as knowing what was originally agreed.

##### — Having studied in New York and developed your career in Belgium, how has your international experience influenced your legal practice and the way you advise clients across different countries?

It was during my studies in New York, particularly thanks to Toni Jaeger-Fine, one of my mentors there, that I discovered the importance of building a network and, just as importantly, how much I enjoy meeting new people and developing professional relationships.

As Cassius has grown, this international dimension has become an essential part of my professional identity.

I genuinely enjoy attending conferences, meeting lawyers from different countries and getting to know them beyond simply exchanging contact details. What matters to me is building relationships of trust with people whose approach to work and professional commitment I know and value.

When a client needs support in another country or in an area requiring different expertise, being able to introduce them to a lawyer whom I know will approach their case with the same rigour and dedication as I would brings me real peace of mind.

Ultimately, what I want for my clients is to ensure that they receive the best possible support, even when this means handing a matter over to someone else or working alongside other professionals.

The international dimension is therefore not an additional feature of my practice. It is an integral part of how I see my profession: being able to rely on trusted colleagues across borders so that a client's support does not stop where my own involvement ends.

##### — You have also further developed your knowledge of international wealth management and succession planning. How important is it to look beyond an immediate legal issue and consider a client's overall circumstances, assets and long-term interests?

For me, the matter entrusted to me is the starting point, but it does not represent the client's entire situation.

It is important to understand the wider context in which it exists: their business, personal and family circumstances, assets, and what they want to protect or build over the long term.

A response to an immediate legal issue should also fit within this broader picture.

This is particularly important when business and family interests are closely connected. The aim is not simply to resolve the issue at hand, but also to consider what a decision means for the future, especially when a client is thinking about the continuity or succession of their business and the transfer of their assets.

In practical terms, I often ask my clients whether they have already put certain basic arrangements in place, such as an extrajudicial protection mandate, a will or a declaration protecting their primary residence from seizure.

These questions help open up the conversation and establish how much consideration they have already given to protecting themselves, their family and their assets beyond their professional activities.

This is also why I place great importance on involving other advisers and specialists, in coordination with the client.

The objective is not to handle everything myself, but to bring together the expertise needed to develop an overall understanding and provide advice that is consistent with the client's priorities.

The client remains at the centre: different areas of expertise should work together around them, rather than each addressing one part of their situation in isolation.

##### — Cassius is now part of the MCSC Group network. Where do you see the main opportunities for collaboration, particularly in supporting athletes, entrepreneurs and international clients whose needs extend beyond a single area of expertise?

I see the MCSC network as an opportunity to make this complementarity of expertise truly practical.

For an athlete, entrepreneur or international client, legal, professional and wealth-related matters rarely arise separately. They are part of the same overall picture and sometimes require coordinated support.

In the equestrian sector, for example, one person may be a rider, an entrepreneur and a horse owner at the same time, with activities or interests in several countries.

The value of a network such as MCSC lies in its ability to bring together the relevant expertise around that individual, according to their needs, rather than leaving them to coordinate all their different advisers on their own.

For me, the value of a network is not simply measured by the number of contacts it offers, but by our ability to work together to create solutions tailored to each client.

##### — What legal or business advice would you give entrepreneurs before they face their first major contractual challenge or commercial dispute?

I would tell them not to wait until a problem arises before surrounding themselves with the right advisers.

We still tend to associate lawyers with the moment when something goes wrong, whereas they can also be valuable partners in building and developing a business on solid foundations.

Before entering into an agreement, entrepreneurs should be willing to ask questions that may sometimes feel uncomfortable when everything is going well: what do we actually expect from each other? What happens if circumstances change or one party wishes to end the collaboration?

This is not about being pessimistic. It is about giving yourself the means to run a business with greater peace of mind.

And if a difficulty does arise, I would advise seeking guidance early rather than carrying the worry alone or allowing the situation to become entrenched.

Ultimately, my advice would be this: protecting your energy to focus on building your business also means knowing when to ask for support.
